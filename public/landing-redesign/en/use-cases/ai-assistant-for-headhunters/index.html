for headhunters, and what can it do?

AI assistant or AI agent: what’s the difference for headhunters?

The terms often get mixed up, and for a headhunter the difference matters in practice. An AI assistant works one exchange at a time: you ask, it answers. It is handy for drafting an email, summarizing an article or explaining an unfamiliar technical term. An AI agent is different. You hand it a goal, and it breaks the goal into steps, searches, reads and compares, and finally hands back the result.

Most of a headhunter’s work is the second kind. Take a single request: “Map the vision teams at Xingchuan Robotics and two peer companies, and see who is already in my database.” Behind it lies a whole chain of searching, reading and checking that no single question and answer can cover. So to judge whether an AI tool is useful to a headhunter, first ask whether it can take on a whole piece of work, not just answer a question.

An AI assistant designed for headhunters adds three things on top. First, it knows what you already have: your clients, your candidates and the org charts you’ve built can all be put to use. Second, when the same person is scattered across different places, it checks the records before merging them. Third, the research it does is kept for the next piece of work, so you don’t have to explain everything from scratch each time.

02

What can AI do for headhunters?

Follow a piece of business from start to finish, and the stages best handed to AI are the ones that mean looking in many places, reading a lot of material and checking things over and over. From finding a client to keeping up with candidates, there are roughly six:

  • Client research for business development (BD): spot companies that are expanding from funding news, careers pages and business moves, and judge whether the need is real and who to approach.
  • Org charts: lay out the departments and reporting lines of target companies and their peers, level by level, and mark where the gaps are.
  • Job matching: break the job description (JD) into must-haves and nice-to-haves, and for each candidate set out why they match and what still needs confirming.
  • Sourcing: search your CV database and org charts first, then fill the gaps from public information, papers and patents, and open-source communities.
  • Identity checks: the same person may be written up differently on a CV, in papers and in open-source communities. Cross-check the records before merging them, and rule out namesakes.
  • Keeping your network current: watch for changes in key candidates’ public career histories and in their teams, so what’s in your database keeps up with time.

These six have one thing in common: they take time, but there is a clear right and wrong. Either you can find it or you can’t. Either it matches or it doesn’t. Handing them to AI is not only about speed. It’s about nothing missed, nothing muddled and everything sourced. You might spend an afternoon tracking down a lead, then by next month forget where you saw it. AI keeps a record of every source.

03

What can’t you hand over to AI?

AI can put the material in order, but some judgments only a person can make. The reason is simple: the answers aren’t in the material at all. They come from talking face to face, from reading people, and from the instinct and trust you’ve built up in the business.

  • Whether someone wants to move

    A team reshuffle or a few years in the same role are only signs. Why they’re looking this time, and what they really care about, you only learn by talking.

  • What the client really wants

    What the JD says and what the hiring manager has in mind are often not the same. Unspoken requirements and the feel of the team are things you have to ask about in person.

  • Whether to recommend, and how to negotiate

    Who to put forward, when to speak up and how to negotiate your fee all involve the trust between you, your client and your candidate. Those calls are yours to make.

  • Respecting candidates

    Use only public information to find people and check identities, and flag what’s uncertain. Whether someone wants to talk, and how much they share, is up to them.

A simple test: can you click through from every finding the AI gives you to the original source? If you can’t see where it came from, treat it as a guess.

04

How do you choose an AI tool for headhunting?

A demo rarely tells you whether a tool is any good. A more reliable way is to test it on something you’ve already finished. You know the answers, so it’s easiest to see where it gets things right and where it’s making things up.

  1. Test it on a finished search

    Pick a search that closed recently, give it the JD you had at the time, and see how the people it finds differ from your list back then.

  2. Open a few sources at random

    Check whether each source really exists, whether it’s about the same person, and whether the information is still current.

  3. See how it handles uncertainty

    A good tool lists what still needs confirming separately, rather than writing guesses up as facts. This matters more than how many people it finds.

  4. See whether it can use your own resources

    Can you bring in your CV database and the org charts you built in Excel? Once they’re in, does it check identities, de-duplicate and merge?

  5. Check the boundaries

    Does it contact people or make promises on your behalf? Who to contact and how to open the conversation should always be your decision.

One more point is easy to overlook: whether the work you’ve done is kept. If the org chart you map today is ready to use when you source next month, the tool gets more useful the more you use it. If not, you start from zero every time.

Where should you start with an AI assistant for headhunters?

Start with something you know well but have never had time to finish. Say, a target company you’ve followed for a long time whose org chart is only half drawn, or a live search whose longlist never feels long enough. On familiar ground, you can tell at a glance whether the results hold up.

The first few times, open the sources and take a look. Treat whatever it marks “To confirm” as your list of questions to ask next. Once you’re comfortable, bring in your CV database and the org charts you’ve made before, so it starts from your own resources. The better it knows what you have, the closer its results come to your business.

And don’t expect it to decide for you. Its job is to put the material in order and make the sources clear. How you read people, what you say and how you look after relationships are still your craft. That is also why clients and candidates keep coming back to you.

What software and tools do headhunters use?

Headhunters usually combine a few kinds of tools: office software and general-purpose AI assistants for writing and organizing material, recruiting platforms for posting roles and finding and contacting candidates, and a recruitment CRM or ATS for tracking clients, roles and where each submission stands. Then there is the AI assistant for headhunters, built for the research before and during a search, such as HunterBuddy from Boseek. Each covers its own stage, and they can be used side by side.

What kinds of AI recruiting agents are there, and which suit headhunters?

AI tools and agents with “recruiting” in the name fall roughly into two groups, by who uses them. One serves in-house HR, helping post roles, screen CVs and schedule interviews. The other serves headhunters, helping with client research, org charts, sourcing and keeping your network current. When choosing, headhunters should look at three things: whether it can use their own CV database, whether every finding has a source, and whether it flags anything uncertain separately. HunterBuddy belongs to the second group and is currently offered as an invitation-only trial.

Will AI replace search consultants?

What AI can take over is mainly research, comparing and organizing. Whether a candidate wants to move, what the client really wants, who to put forward and how to negotiate the fee all depend on judging people and on mutual trust, and none of that is in any document. Headhunters who know how to use AI can spend more time on these things.

Can you use an AI assistant’s results as they are?

Treat them as sourced guidance, not conclusions. A good AI assistant for headhunters names the source of every finding and marks anything without a reliable source “To confirm”. How big a team a candidate leads now, or whether they’re open to a move, you only learn by talking, and that is for you to confirm.

Industry primer:
from an unfamiliar sector to a confident first call

The industry primer is a capability that organizes how a value chain, its products, companies and roles fit together into an industry map you can talk from. It calibrates its understanding of roles against real JDs, and produces an editable mind map.

HunterBuddy doesn’t produce an industry research report you forget as soon as you’ve read it. It helps you build a picture of the industry you can explain in your own words, then tells you who to look for and what to ask.

Boseek·Updated 2026-11-21

01

What’s really hard about entering a new industry?

  • Industry knowledge has to be built from zero

    What the product is, how the technical work is divided and why each role exists all have to be learned from the start. Terms, processes and company types arrive all at once, and it’s hard to see the whole picture quickly.

  • An unclear value chain, and roles you can’t place up- or downstream

    Who designs, who manufactures, who holds the core components and who ultimately pays is hard to sort out fast. Without understanding the hand-offs, it’s easy to treat roles with the same title at different companies as the same job.

  • Not speaking the business language breaks both the JD and the candidate profile

    The same thing goes by different names at different companies, and JDs are full of abbreviations and internal terms. Bring search terms from your old industry into a new one, and you neither find the right people nor understand their CVs.

  • Deep mapping takes a long time to pay off

    A new industry comes with no network and no talent pool. Working out which companies hold the key people takes long groundwork, and results don’t show up quickly.

  • Hard to tell whether someone really did the work

    Under the same title, one person owns the architecture and another only executes. Going by keywords alone, it’s easy to overestimate the scope of someone’s responsibilities and their technical depth.

  • Without the jargon and the unwritten rules, the inner circle stays closed

    Which experience counts as core, which projects carry weight and which questions not to ask are never written in a JD. Without knowing them, even when you find the right person, it’s hard to start the conversation.

02

How HunterBuddy gets you into a new industry fast

  1. Plain words first, then the terms

    It starts from one value chain, upstream and downstream: what problem it solves, how it gets made, which companies do the work and who finally buys. It explains how the business works first, then adds the terms and English abbreviations.

  2. One value chain, showing how companies divide the work

    The industry map is layered by product type and job family. For each kind of company, it explains what they do, what they control, what they outsource and how they make money.

  3. Real JDs turned into a map of roles

    It breaks JDs down layer by layer, from the product all the way to evidence of results. For each role, it explains the actual work, common titles, departmental responsibilities, hand-offs up- and downstream and the roles it’s easily confused with.

  4. Candidate profiles and transferable paths

    The industry map connects the key roles and the typical paths people take between them. You know which companies to look at, which words to search with and what kind of background to look for.

  5. People judged on evidence, not titles

    When judging a candidate, it looks at both their understanding of the product and the evidence of their results. It never lets a title or skill keywords stand in for the real responsibilities.

  6. An editable industry mind map

    Finally, it produces an editable mind map that runs from the product architecture to search keywords. When there’s a lot to cover, it can be split into several sub-maps, easy to keep adding to and to share.

Where it stops

HunterBuddy won’t pretend to know an industry for you, and won’t present inferences as market facts. It gives you the structure first, then tells you who to look for, how to judge and what to ask.
